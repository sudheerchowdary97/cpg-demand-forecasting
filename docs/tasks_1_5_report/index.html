The opportunity

For a business the size of PepsiCo (~$91B revenue, 20+ billion-dollar brands, 200+ markets), even a small accuracy gain compounds into large value. Poor forecasts cause two expensive problems — empty shelves and wasted stock. Better forecasts fix both.

Why it matters — five costly problems
🛒

Stockouts

Empty shelves lose the sale and send shoppers to rivals — around 4% of revenue for typical retailers.

🗑️

Waste & freshness

Snacks and chilled drinks spoil; overstock becomes markdowns and write-offs that erode margin.

🚚

Delivery complexity

Direct-store-delivery routes must stock the right amount per store, per day, across many markets.

🎯

Promotions & events

Promos, holidays and heatwaves cause big local swings. Miss the spike → stockout; over-forecast → waste.

✨

New launches

New flavors and new-market entries have little history — "cold-start" forecasting is hard and high-stakes.

📈

The payoff

Fewer stockouts, less waste, higher service levels and faster rollouts — better top line and bottom line.

Data & framing

First, turn the business problem into a precise, measurable target — then build a realistic dataset that mirrors how PepsiCo actually sells: many brands, many store types, four very different markets.

What we predict

Daily sales (units) for every product in every store, 28 days ahead, with a confidence range — scored separately for each market so local differences are respected.

How the market is structured

Geography → sales channel → retailer → store → product. Channels differ hugely: small "general trade" shops dominate India & Pakistan (and have no till data), while supermarkets dominate the UK & Australia.

Turning data into signals

Models don't read raw spreadsheets — they need engineered "signals." We built these carefully so a forecast for any day only ever uses information that was actually known at that point in time (no accidental peeking at the future, which would flatter the results).

The signals we feed the model

  • Recent sales history — the last few weeks of demand and its trend
  • Calendar — day of week, month, holidays, season
  • Price & promotions — current price and discount depth
  • Product & place — brand, category, store, channel, market

Why you can trust the numbers

  • Every signal is computed causally — only from the past.
  • Categories are learned on training data only, so results aren't inflated.
  • Testing always uses later dates than training — like forecasting for real.

Setting the bar

How we grade a forecast

WRMSSE is the headline score (the industry-standard M5 metric). In plain terms: it measures error, scaled so big and small products compare fairly, and weighted by each product's recent sales value — so the score reflects what matters commercially. Supporting metrics (MAE, RMSE, MAPE, sMAPE, WAPE) give complementary views. Lower is better.

Under the hood — architecture, training & full metrics

Architecture

  • Input: flattened 28-day sales window (log1p) + 11 calendar/price covariates
  • Embeddings for market/channel/retailer/store/brand/category/SKU (dim min(50, 1.6·vocab⁰·⁵⁶))
  • MLP [256,128] + BatchNorm + Dropout 0.1

Training (PyTorch Lightning)

  • Huber loss on log1p; AdamW (lr 1e-3, wd 1e-5)
  • ReduceLROnPlateau + early stopping; batch 1024
  • Leakage-free recursive rollout; fit_scope=global (fit once, score per market)

Strategy & roadmap

The guiding principle: don't assume, prove. We start with simple local models, add a shared global model, compare them market by market, and only adopt more complex approaches where the evidence says they help.

The approach, step by step
1

Local baselines

Simple per-market models — the bar every AI model must beat.

2

Global model

One shared network with product/market embeddings; handles new products.

3

Compare per market

Backtests decide how much a shared model actually helps — no assumptions.

4

Hybrid refinement

Segment or fine-tune per region only where it demonstrably wins.

Built for the real world. In production, data-residency rules (e.g. India's DPDP Act) may require region-segmented models or federated learning — the staged approach accommodates that.
